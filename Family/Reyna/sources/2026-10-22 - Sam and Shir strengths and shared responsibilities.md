# Sam and Shir: strengths and shared responsibilities

Sam asked in this chat: “from what u know about me, how can me and shir play off our strengths”. These are proposed discussion points, not an agreed division of work or a clinical assessment.

## Evidence and limits

- Sam has content-design experience simplifying complex problems, and this conversation shows him preparing questions and pursuing care. Problem framing, writing, and advocacy are plausible strengths. Reliable sustained execution cannot be inferred from them.
- October 21 intake records that Shir teaches, does most grocery shopping and about 90% of cooking, and needs physical support. Sam handles finances with autopay and participates in parenting. These responsibilities do not prove either person's preferences or spare capacity.
- Both partners have health-related limits. Shir's perspective and self-described strengths were not gathered here. No live Gmail, Drive, or calendar review was performed.
- Duplicate search found no dedicated strengths/division-of-work note. Newer October 21 intake supersedes the older therapy account of the paused intake: the intake did occur.

## Proposed one-week experiment

1. Keep Sam's contributions bounded and concrete: one defined task with a visible finish, such as drafting one message, checking one bill, or completing one manageable household task. Choose according to actual energy.
2. Ask Shir which current responsibilities fit her strengths and which drain her. Do not convert her existing load into evidence she should keep all of it.
3. Use a shared visible list with task, owner, due date, and backup. Reminders and task status belong in the system rather than Shir's memory.
4. Check capacity before time-sensitive commitments. Agree on an early backup trigger so neither partner has to abandon obligations at the last minute.
5. Have a ten-minute weekly check-in: one priority each, one household priority, and what to simplify or ask someone else to do. A proposed experiment, not new homework assigned by a clinician.

Suggested opening: “I want to contribute in ways you can count on, without making you manage me. What do I do that helps you most, what is wearing you down, and what could we each own this week?”

### Revised interpretation and practical proposals

Both partners show creativity, care and advocacy. Sam demonstrates research, explanation, opportunity finding and personal connection. Shir demonstrates creative teaching, practical problem definition and coordination. These are observations from examples, not fixed personality labels.

1. Pair bounded research by Sam with joint choice against explicit criteria. Shir should not become automatic project manager or quality checker.
2. For housing/finances, preserve Shir's concrete questions and use Sam's research to prepare one page for an adviser. Outsource calculations and consequential review rather than assume Sam's historical financial ownership means unlimited current capacity.
3. Scheduling needs a shared rule: proposed until both confirm availability, childcare and access. One person sends the final confirmation after agreement. Recheck details against calendar/system, not Shir's memory.
4. Recognize existing labor before adding duties. Ask what task Shir would actually like removed. Sam can own one manageable practical result or arranging outside help, with external reminders and support.
5. Reconnect through art, music and curiosity using a small, accessible activity. Sam can contribute a short story/caption/photo selection and Shir can contribute art interpretation or a playful prompt if she wants. This is time together, not a proposed business or recovery performance test.
6. Use friends, family and existing support for discrete agreed jobs so the partnership does not depend on each being the other's only backup.
