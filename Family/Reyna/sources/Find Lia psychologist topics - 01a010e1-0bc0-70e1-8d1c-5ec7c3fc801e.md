Yes. These are the big ones. I’d frame them as one connected pattern: **Lia needs connection and control, and your TBI makes consistency much harder.**

**1. How My TBI Affects Lia**
Tell Rayna:

> “Lia can tell my brain is different. She notices when I forget things, lose focus, need to rest, or can’t answer her. Sometimes she says she’ll ask Ima/Mom instead. I’m worried she is absorbing my limitations as instability, rejection, or something she needs to manage.”

Ask Rayna:

- How do we explain my TBI to a 3- or 4-year-old in a way that is honest but not scary?
- What should I say when I’m too depleted to play, answer, or engage?
- How do I reassure her that my rest/brain problems are not her fault?
- What signs should we watch for that she is feeling anxious, over-responsible, or insecure because of my condition?

Useful wording for Lia might be something like:

> “Abba’s brain gets tired faster now. I love you all the time, even when I need quiet or rest. Grown-ups are taking care of it. You don’t have to fix it.”

**2. Attention And Caving**
Tell Rayna:

> “When Lia wants my attention, I often cave because I’m exhausted, guilty, or trying to avoid a meltdown. I become the peacemaker. Then I worry I’m teaching her that if she pushes hard enough, I’ll give in.”

This is not just “spoiling.” It is a TBI capacity problem plus a parenting pattern. You may be trying to buy calm because your nervous system has no margin.

Ask Rayna:

- What should I do when Lia wants attention but I truly have no capacity?
- How do I give her enough connection without making myself the on-demand entertainment system?
- What are good default scripts I can use when my brain is too tired to improvise?
- How do I repair after I snap, withdraw, or cave?

Possible script:

> “I want to be with you. My brain needs 10 minutes. You can sit next to me with a book, or you can play with blocks. Then I’ll come back.”

The key question for Rayna:

> “How do I stay loving without letting Lia run the whole emotional weather of the house?”

**3. Control Battles**
Tell Rayna:

> “A lot of conflicts seem to be about control, not the actual object. Meals, bath, shower, getting dressed, climbing routes, transitions. If I offer two choices, she sometimes rejects both and invents a third. She wants it to be her idea.”

This may be normal preschool autonomy, but your notes suggest it is becoming a repeated household pattern.

Ask Rayna:

- How much control should we intentionally give her?
- When should we hold the boundary without negotiating?
- Is the best strategy to preview transitions earlier so she has time to ‘choose’ the plan?
- How do we avoid turning every routine into a debate?

A useful structure:

> “The boundary is fixed. The small choice is hers.”

Examples:

- “It is bath night. You choose bubbles or no bubbles.”
- “We are leaving the park. You choose skipping or holding hands.”
- “It is time to eat. You choose the blue spoon or the yellow spoon.”

Rayna may help you separate:

- **Real choice:** clothes, spoon, book, route, toy.
- **Parent decision:** bedtime, bath, leaving, safety, screen limits.

**10. Parenting With Low Brain Capacity**
This is probably the most important one.

Tell Rayna:

> “I need a parenting system that works with my TBI, not a perfect-parent plan that assumes I have normal executive function. I need scripts, routines, and rules simple enough to use when I’m tired.”

Ask Rayna:

- What are 3 household rules/routines we should make extremely consistent?
- What scripts should I memorize for low-capacity moments?
- How do Shir and I stay aligned so Lia gets the same response from both of us?
- What should I stop trying to solve in the moment?
- What is the minimum effective parenting plan for a depleted brain?

A good request:

> “Can you help us make a tiny playbook for mornings, meals, bath, bedtime, transitions, and Abba-needs-rest moments?”

The goal is not to become stricter. The goal is to become **predictable**. Predictability will probably help both Lia and your brain.
